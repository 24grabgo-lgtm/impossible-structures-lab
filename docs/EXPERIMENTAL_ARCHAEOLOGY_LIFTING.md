# Experimental Archaeology — Lifting Techniques

**Status:** Research reference for Impossible Structures Lab  
**Use:** Baseline method flags for uprights + lintels (Stonehenge-class trilithon PoC)

---

## 1. What experimental archaeology actually tested

People have moved and raised multi-tonne concrete/stone proxies with **period-plausible gear**: timber, rope, levers, ramps, labor. Results do **not** prove “this is how Stonehenge was built”; they prove **these methods can work under stated conditions**.

---

## 2. Raising uprights

### A. Ramped stone-hole + tip (classic)
1. Dig socket: **one vertical face** (stop), **one sloping ramp**.  
2. Drag stone base-first over the ramp edge until it tips into the hole and rests on the slope.  
3. Haul the head upright with ropes; pack rubble to lock.  

Often combined with **shear legs / A-frame** so the pull angle is efficient (rope over apex → more vertical component).

**Experiments:** Stone (1924 scale), later full-ish scale concrete work (e.g. 1990s BBC/English Heritage line of demos); Historic Concepts / English Heritage public monolith trials (rollers → tip into ramped pit → A-frame raise).

### B. A-frame / shear-leg levering without pure direct pull
Horizontal crew force alone is often not enough; **A-frame multiplies / redirects**. Richards & Whitby-type work showed A-frame levering + packing between pulls can crank a ~40 t class upright toward vertical when direct pull fails.

**Constraint (British Museum / Pitts):** On a crowded building site (inner trilithons first), long rope runs and big sheerlegs may not fit. So “empty field A-frame” is not automatic for every stone.

### C. Rock / lever + pack (Rapa Nui–inspired)
Rock the monolith slightly with levers; **pack timber or rubble under the raised side**; repeat. Mound grows under the head until near-vertical; light rope finish. Parallel to Gowland’s lever ideas at Stonehenge and Heyerdahl-era Rapa Nui demos.

**Game value:** Slow, safe-feeling, low peak crew; high timber consumption.

### D. Low-crew pivot / “forgotten technology” demos (Wallington-class)
Modern experimental builders (e.g. Wally Wallington) show **counterweighted levers and pivots** moving/erecting multi-ton concrete with one or few people. Powerful for sandbox flags; **not** mainstream archaeological consensus as *the* Stonehenge method — label as **experimental / low-crew leverage**.

### E. Socket + counterweight tilt (dolmen-scale research)
Menga-type analyses emphasize tipping into sockets with **moving counterweight** for smoother control. Useful as a precision-placement variant.

## 3. Raising lintels (the hard wow)

| Method | How it works | Pros | Cons |
|--------|--------------|------|------|
| **Timber crib** | Lever one end → slide timber → alternate ends → stack grows under stone until lintel height → slide onto uprights | Works in tight space; famous textbook method | Huge timber; unstable mid-stack; slow |
| **Earth/timber ramp** | Build ramp against uprights; haul lintel up greased/tracked slope (A-frame assist) | Demonstrated (e.g. Whitby ~10 t lintel on 30° ramp with large crew; Pavel ~5 t with levers on timber ramp, small crew) | Massive fill; footprint; may clash with standing stones |
| **Scaffold / platform** | Not full surround; partial scaffold + pull | Shown workable in trials | Timber + geometry skill |
| **Opposing levers on beams** | Pavel-style dual lever systems on sloping beams | Low crew counts in one experiment | Needs rehearsal; scaling questions |

Richards & Whitby: both **ramp and crib** can work; pick by labor, timber, and site crowding.

## 4. Moving before the lift (feeds the raise)

| Technique | Note |
|-----------|------|
| **Sled + lubrication** (water, fat, clay) | Classic friction cut |
| **Rollers / sleepers** | Common demo; roller management is skill |
| **“Stone rowing” (Pipes)** | Lever-lift clear of supports, shift sideways, set down — small steps, less pure drag |
| **Greased timber rails** | Sledge on prepared track |
| **Barges** | Only if water segment exists on map |

Haul experiments (e.g. ~40 t class on gentle slopes) often need **order-of ~100+ people** depending on friction and grade — or fewer with better mechanical advantage.

---

## 5. Numbers worth remembering (order-of-magnitude)

- Sarsen uprights: up to ~**40 t** class; lintels often **~5–20 t** depending on which stone.  
- Public trials sometimes use **~4 t** modern monoliths for safety/repeatability.  
- Pavel: **~5 t** lintel, **~10 people**, days, lever/ramp hybrid.  
- Whitby-style: **~10 t** lintel, **~90 people**, hours on prepared ramp + A-frame.  
- A-frame team generating **~12 t** pull force cited in one British Academy write-up context.  

Treat as **scenario parameters**, not gospel.

## 7. Design law for the game

**Every lift method should fail legibly** (tip past plumb, crib collapse, ramp shear, rope stretch, socket blow-out) so the Kerbal loop holds.
